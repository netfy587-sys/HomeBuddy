MILE 0START

A marathon is, on paper, a simple thing: a single race over a fixed distance. In practice, it sits at the crossing point of ancient myth, Olympic history, exercise physiology, and one of the largest participation movements in sport.

This page walks through that whole story — where the distance came from, what actually happens inside the body over 26.2 miles, how the world's biggest races came to be, and how fast a human being has ever managed to run it. Nothing here is for sale; it's simply an account of the race.

MILE 2THE LEGEND

An uneven distance, fixed by a stadium

The marathon's name comes from the Greek town of Marathon, roughly 25 miles from Athens. According to legend recorded centuries after the fact, a runner named Pheidippides carried news of a Greek victory over Persia there in 490 BCE, delivered his message, and collapsed. Historians generally treat the story as embellishment layered onto an earlier, less dramatic account by Herodotus — but the image stuck.

When the first modern Olympics were held in Athens in 1896, organizers created a race retracing that route, roughly 40 kilometres, as a tribute to Greek history. A Greek runner, Spyridon Louis, won it in front of a home crowd, and the event became an instant centerpiece of the Games.

The odd 26.2-mile figure came later, almost by accident. At the 1908 London Olympics, the course was stretched so the race could start at Windsor Castle and finish in front of the royal viewing box at White City Stadium — a distance of 26 miles and 385 yards. That specific length wasn't made standard until 1921, when it was adopted as the official distance going forward.

MILE 8THE MACHINE

What the body is actually doing

For the first hour or so, a trained runner's muscles draw energy mostly from glycogen — sugar stored in the muscles and liver — supplemented by fat. The pace a runner can sustain for a full marathon sits close to their lactate threshold: the point where the body can still clear lactate roughly as fast as it produces it, before fatigue accelerates.

Two numbers dominate the science of distance running. VO2 max measures how much oxygen the body can use during hard effort — it sets a ceiling on potential. Running economy measures how efficiently a runner converts oxygen into forward motion at a given pace — it's a large part of why two runners with similar VO2 max can finish minutes apart. Elite marathoners tend to combine a high ceiling with exceptional economy, built over years of training.

Cardiovascular adaptation matters just as much: a trained distance runner's heart pumps more blood per beat, and their muscles grow denser with the capillaries and mitochondria that turn oxygen into usable energy.

MILE 13.1HALFWAY

Getting to the start line ready

Most marathon training plans run 12 to 20 weeks and build around one central rule: gradually increasing total distance while keeping most runs at an easy, conversational pace. Roughly 80% of a trained runner's weekly mileage is often run well below race pace — hard efforts are deliberately rationed, because the body adapts during recovery, not during the workout itself.

A weekly long run — building toward 18 to 22 miles for many recreational runners — teaches the body to spare glycogen and burn fat more efficiently at speed. In the final two to three weeks before race day, runners "taper": mileage drops sharply while intensity stays, letting the legs fully recover without losing sharpness.

Fueling strategy has become part of training itself. Many competitive runners now practice taking in 60–90 grams of carbohydrate per hour during long runs, training the gut to absorb fuel at a rate that would have caused stomach distress a generation ago.

MILE 20THE WALL

Where the race actually begins

Runners have talked about "hitting the wall" for as long as the modern marathon has existed — a sudden, severe drop in pace that tends to arrive around mile 18 to 20. It isn't in the runner's head, though the mind is where it's fought.

Glycogen depletion

The body stores roughly 90–120 minutes of glycogen at marathon effort. Once stores run low, the body shifts harder toward fat for fuel — a much slower process — and pace falls even if effort stays the same.

Muscle-level fatigue

Beyond fuel, the repeated pounding of 20-plus miles causes microscopic muscle damage, altering stride mechanics and making each subsequent mile measurably harder on the joints and connective tissue.

Carbohydrate loading

The days-before practice of eating more carbohydrates while tapering training raises glycogen stores, pushing the point of depletion later into the race — sometimes past the finish line entirely.

Pacing discipline

The single most reliable defense is even pacing. Runners who go out even slightly too fast in the first half are disproportionately likely to fade hard in the last six miles.

MILE 24THE MAJORS

Six races, one circuit

Since 1897

Boston

The oldest annual marathon in the world, run every Patriots' Day. It's also the only major that still requires most entrants to hit a qualifying time.

Since 1970

New York City

Began with 127 runners looping Central Park; now one of the largest marathons on Earth, routed through all five boroughs.

Since 1974

Chicago

Famous for its flat, fast course through the Loop — the site of several men's world records, including Kelvin Kiptum's in 2023.

Since 1981

London

Known for elite fields and enormous charity fundraising — a large share of finishers run it to raise money for a cause.

Since 1974

Berlin

The record-breaker's course: its long straightaways and flat profile have hosted more men's world records than any other major.

Since 2007

Tokyo

The youngest of the six and the last to join the World Marathon Majors series, in 2013, completing the current circuit.

A hundred years of getting faster

MILE 26.2FINISH

A race mostly run by amateurs

For every elite athlete chasing a world record, there are tens of thousands of people running for entirely different reasons. The modern marathon boom — driven heavily by major-city races — has turned the distance into one of the most common items on a bucket list, with charity fundraising now a core part of races like London and New York.

Finish times for the field as a whole have gradually slowed even as elite times have dropped, a reflection of just how many first-time and mid-pack runners now take part. The gap between the fastest human ever to run 26.2 miles and an average finisher has never been wider — and the race has never been more popular.
